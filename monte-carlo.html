Method and assumptions

How a single simulated trial runs

  • Scope is drawn at random from the range you entered, then multiplied by a random split rate between your low and high bounds.
  • For epics, each epic's item count is drawn independently between its min and max, after the domain understanding multipliers stretch that range.
  • Each sprint draws one throughput value at random from your historical samples. Historical data already contains good sprints and bad ones, which is what produces the spread.
  • That draw is multiplied by team focus, then by the availability factor for the month the sprint starts in, then by the seasonal reduction if that month is ticked.
  • A month flagged “floor at min historical” ignores the random draw and uses the lowest value in your sample set instead.
  • Capacity is consumed against epics in priority order; leftover capacity within a sprint flows to the next epic.
  • The trial ends when scope reaches zero. Thousands of trials produce the distribution the percentiles are read from.

Reading the numbers

  • 85% confidence means 85 out of 100 simulated trials finished on or before that date. This is the number to commit to.
  • 95% is the conservative commitment used where missing the date is expensive.
  • 50% is a coin flip. It is shown for reference only and should never be quoted as a plan.
  • The cut line is the last epic whose 85% date still lands on or before the deadline. Everything below it is at genuine risk.

Known limits

  • Historical throughput is assumed to remain representative. A reorganisation, a new domain or a team change invalidates it.
  • Dependencies between epics are not modelled. If epic 4 blocks epic 2, the real answer is worse than this shows.
  • Items are treated as interchangeable units of delivery. Throughput forecasting works because item sizes average out over a sprint, not because they are equal.
  • The month of a sprint is taken from its start date.
